6 things that matter more
than your tech stack

A practical guide for engineers job hunting in 2026 — what actually moves the needle when the stack on your resume isn't the deciding factor.

Ajay KolagantiSenior Java / Spring Boot Engineer7+ yrs

After 7+ years across finance, telecom, and insurance — and now job hunting again myself — the pattern is consistent: the engineers who stand out aren't the ones with the trendiest stack. They're the ones who can talk about their work in a way that makes an interviewer trust them. This guide breaks down the six things that consistently matter more than which framework is on your resume.

01 /

Can you explain a decision, not just describe what you built?

Anyone can list what they built. Fewer people can explain why they built it that way — and that's what interviewers are actually probing for.

Before the interview, pick 2–3 projects and write down the key decisions you made: why this database, why this pattern, why this tradeoff.
Practice saying the decision out loud in one sentence, then the reasoning in two more.
If you inherited the decision (legacy code, team standard), say so honestly — and explain what you'd evaluate if you owned the call.
EXAMPLE

"We used optimistic locking instead of pessimistic because our write contention was low but read volume was high — pessimistic would've throttled reads for a problem we rarely had."

02 /

Do you know WHY your system was designed that way, not just how?

"How" is implementation. "Why" is judgment. Senior-level interviews are almost always testing for the second one.

For any system you've worked on, be ready to name the constraint that shaped it — scale, team size, deadline, legacy dependency, compliance requirement.
Avoid pure feature-walkthroughs. Every explanation should connect back to a constraint or goal.
If you don't know why a legacy decision was made, that's a fine answer too — say what you'd ask to find out.
EXAMPLE

"The service was monolithic because at the time we had one team and shipping speed mattered more than independent scaling — splitting it would've added coordination overhead with zero payoff."

03 /

Can you talk numbers — scale, latency, cost — not just features?

Numbers signal you understood the impact of your work, not just the mechanics of it. Vague claims ("improved performance") read as junior; specific ones read as senior.

For each major project, find at least one real number: requests/sec, latency before/after, cost saved, error rate reduced, team size, data volume.
If exact numbers aren't available, use defensible estimates and say they're estimates.
Numbers work best paired with the decision that produced them — the number alone isn't the story.
EXAMPLE

"Adding a caching layer cut average response time from ~800ms to under 120ms on our highest-traffic endpoint, and reduced DB load enough that we delayed a planned read-replica purchase."

04 /

Do you ask good questions in the interview, or just answer them?

The questions you ask reveal how you think about a system before you've even touched it — and they're one of the few moments you control entirely.

Prepare 3–4 questions in advance: about team structure, current pain points, what "success" looks like in 6 months, and a technical question about their actual stack or scale.
Avoid questions answerable by a 30-second Google search (those signal low effort).
Listen to the answers — a good follow-up question is more impressive than a long prepared one.
EXAMPLE

"What's the biggest technical pain point your team is dealing with right now that this role is meant to help solve?"

05 /

Is your resume telling a story, or just listing technologies?

A resume that's a list of nouns (Java, Spring Boot, AWS, Kafka) is forgettable. A resume that shows a trajectory — what you owned, what changed because of you — is not.

For each role, lead bullets with outcomes and ownership, not tool names. Tools can be a secondary line or skills section.
Cut anything that doesn't support the story of the role you're applying for now — tailoring beats completeness.
Read it as a stranger would: does it answer "what did this person actually change?" in under 10 seconds per role?
EXAMPLE

Weak: "Worked with Java, Spring Boot, AWS, SQL Server." Strong: "Rebuilt a legacy batch process into an event-driven service, cutting nightly processing time from 4 hours to 35 minutes."

06 /

Are you targeting roles that actually fit, or just applying everywhere?

Volume-applying to anything with the right keyword is a numbers game that mostly produces fatigue. Fit-based targeting produces better conversations, faster.

Before applying, check that the role's actual requirements (not just the title) match your real experience — title inflation is common on both sides.
For roles requiring sponsorship, verify the sponsorship signal is real (recent filings, explicit language) rather than relying on a checkbox alone.
Track what you apply to and why — patterns in what gets responses tell you more than any single rejection does.
EXAMPLE

A "Senior Java Engineer" posting that's really a mid-level support role in disguise wastes both sides' time — reading the actual day-to-day responsibilities, not just the title, avoids that mismatch.

// THE TAKEAWAY

None of this means the stack doesn't matter — it gets you past the resume filter. But once you're in the room, the stack stops being the differentiator. How you reason about decisions, numbers, and fit is what does.

Save this. Run through it before your next interview.
